This Unit at a Glance

Your student already knows tenths and hundredths. This unit adds one more place, thousandths, and then uses all three: reading and writing decimals, finding the value of each digit, comparing and rounding, and multiplying and dividing by 10, 100 and 1,000. It ends with metric measurement and mental math with decimals.

The big idea

Each place is worth 10 times as much as the place to its right. Divide 1 into 10 equal parts and you get tenths. Divide a tenth into 10 and you get hundredths. Divide again and you get thousandths.

The story

This unit is The Great Pyramid. Your student works beside Hemiunu, the real master builder of King Khufu's pyramid about 4,500 years ago (shown in an illustration). Each lesson is one day on the build site, from staking the base to setting the capstone. The history is real; the measurements are practice numbers.

How We Teach It

Your student first builds each number with a model on the screen: a meter stick divided into smaller and smaller equal parts, place-value discs, number lines. Then they see it on a place-value chart, and only then write the numbers. The picture we come back to all unit is the place-value chart, in the same colors as the Dimensions Math books:

Ones5.Tenths3Hundredths1Thousandths8
⬅ One place left: 10 times as much
➡ One place right: 110 as much
A 0 holds an empty place: 0.038 is not 0.38.

Two ways to read a decimal. 0.526 is "zero point five two six" or "five hundred twenty-six thousandths." The second way shows the place value, so we use both.

Mix-Ups to Watch For

  • A missing zero. 38 mm is 38 thousandths of a meter: 0.038 m, not 0.38 m.
  • "More digits means bigger." 0.5 > 0.483, because 5 tenths is more than 4 tenths. Write 0.5 as 0.500 to see it.
  • Comparing like whole numbers. 0.48 > 0.135, even though 48 is less than 135. Compare place by place from the left.
  • Rounding twice. To the nearest tenth, 3.649 ≈ 3.6. Look only at the digit right after the rounding place.
  • "Just add a zero." 2.5 × 10 is 25, not 2.50. The digits move one place left.
🏠

Try It at Home

  1. Kitchen scale. Weigh an apple or a bag of rice in grams. Write the weight in kilograms (for example, 186 g = 0.186 kg).
  2. Ruler hunt. Measure small things in millimeters, then say each length in meters, both ways: "zero point zero four two" and "forty-two thousandths."
  3. Race times. Look up winning times from a swim meet or track race. Which time is fastest? Put three of them in order.
  4. Receipt rounding. Round each price on a grocery receipt to the nearest dollar, then to the nearest tenth of a dollar (10 cents).
  5. Decimal War. Each player flips 3 cards (ace = 1; 10s and face cards = 0) and builds the greatest number they can as 0.___. Read both numbers aloud; the greater one wins the round.

The best question to ask at home: "What is that digit worth?"
